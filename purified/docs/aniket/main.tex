\subsection{Notation}
We let bold symbols denote vectors, and reserve $\mathbf{x}_t$ for model activations at position $t$ in a sequence, $\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{(x_1,...,x_T)}$ for a sequence of activations in a temporal window of length $T$ $\mathbf{u}$ for feature activations. We let $d$ denote the dimension of the residual stream, so that $\mathbf{x}_t\in\mathbb{R}^d$, and $H$ denote the hidden dimension of an autoencoder architecture so that $\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^H$.
\subsection{Temporal Crosscoders}
\label{sec:txc}
% TODO: a bit awkward might be worth rephrasing.
% The fundamental limitation of SAE-based architectures for modelling temporal correlations is that they use a stationary sparse code to model language activations \cite{lubana2025priors}.
The core feature of a temporal crosscoder is that is uses a \textit{shared} set of latents $\mathbf{u}$ to reconstruct the activations for an entire temporal window $\mathbf{X}$.
% A temporal crosscoder is thus an acausal crosscoder in which the objects being crosscoded are sequence positions rather than layers.
Given a window of residual-stream activations of size $T$:
\[
    \mathbf{X}_t=(\mathbf{x}_t,\ldots\mathbf{x}_{t+T}),
\]
we learn a single sparse latent vector $\mathbf{u}_t\in\mathbb{R}^H$ shared across all positions in the window:
\[
    \mathbf{u}_t
    =
    \sigma\!\left(
        \sum_{\tau=1}^{T}
        W^{\tau}_{\mathrm{enc}}\mathbf{x}_{t+\tau}
        + \mathbf{b}_{\mathrm{enc}}
    \right).
\]
The latent vector is then decoded with position-specific decoders to reconstruct each activation in the window,
\[
    \hat{\mathbf{x}}_{t+\tau}
    =
    W^{\tau}_{\mathrm{dec}}\mathbf{u}_t
    +
    \mathbf{b}^{\tau}_{\mathrm{dec}}.
\]
Thus a temporal crosscoder is the temporal analogue of a layerwise acausal crosscoder~\cite{lindsey2024sparsecrosscoders}: the layer index is replaced by a relative position index.
% The central architectural choice is the shared latent space.
% Re-write this to be about the ability to choose the scale
A feature may reconstruct primarily one position, recovering the behavior of a local SAE, or it may have decoder support across several positions, representing a temporally extended pattern.

We train temporal crosscoders with a reconstruction loss over the full window, together with explicit sparsity from the latent activation function. In our default implementation, $\sigma$ is BatchTopK~\cite{bussmann2024batchtopksparseautoencoders}, a variant of TopK~\cite{gao2024scaling}, applied to the shared latent vector for each window, and we set decoder biases to zero so that reconstructive mass is assigned to features rather than biases. Our base temporal crosscoder uses only this acausal window-reconstruction objective. In the larger TXC-pro variant, we additionally include two temporal inductive biases: a Matryoshka objective over nested latent groups, encouraging useful feature dictionaries at multiple effective widths, and a multi-distance contrastive loss over shifted windows, encouraging features that describe the same temporal pattern to align across nearby offsets. Unless otherwise stated, temporal crosscoders and the SAE/crosscoder baselines are trained on the same activation cache and compared at fixed hookpoint, expansion factor, and sparsity.

Synthetic datasets make it possible to evaluate feature recovery against known ground-truth features \cite{chanin2025synthetic}. Existing synthetic benchmarks define feature activations independently at each sequence position, so the ground-truth feature structure is local. To benchmark temporal architectures, we generalize this setting by making the feature-firing pattern a stochastic process across sequence positions.

A synthetic activation sequence
\[
    \mathbf{X}=(\mathbf{x}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{x}_T),
    \qquad
    \mathbf{x}_t\in\mathbb{R}^d,
\]
is specified by three pieces of data. First, a set of $N$ ground-truth feature directions
\[
    \mathcal{F}=\{\mathbf{f}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{f}_N\},
    \qquad
    \mathbf{f}_i\in\mathbb{R}^d.
\]
Second, a probability distribution $P_T$ over binary feature-firing masks
\[
    A=(\mathbf{a}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{a}_T)\in(\{0,1\}^N)^T,\qquad
    \mathbf{a}_t=(a_{t,1},\ldots,a_{t,N}),
\]
where $a_{t,i}=1$ indicates that feature $i$ fires at position $t$. Third, a distribution $M_T$ over per-position firing magnitudes, taken to be i.i.d.\ folded-normal across feature index and sequence position, $m_{t,i}=|\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^2)|$ with $\mu=1.0$ and $\sigma=0.15$. The synthetic activation at position $t$ is
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{x}_t=\sum_{i=1}^N a_{t,i}\,m_{t,i}\,\mathbf{f}_i.
    \label{eq:synthetic-activation}
\end{equation}
The conventional, local synthetic setting is recovered as the special case in which $P_T$ factorizes across sequence positions, $P_T(\mathbf{a}_{1:T})=\prod_{t=1}^T p_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbf{a}_t)$. Local feature correlations are encoded by the one-position marginal over $\mathbf{a}_t$; temporal correlations are encoded by the joint distribution over $\mathbf{a}_{1:T}$.

The HMM presentation then generates the distribution over firing patterns as a sequence of updates over the initial state vector:
\[
    P_T(\mathbf{a}_{1:T})
    =
    \boldsymbol{\eta}^{\emptyset\top}
    T^{(\mathbf{a}_1)}
    T^{(\mathbf{a}_2)}
    \cdots
    T^{(\mathbf{a}_T)}
    \mathbf{1}.
\]
A dictionary learning architecture may hence learn to recover the \textit{local} feature firing probabilities $a_{i,t}$ or it may learn to recover the \textit{global} latents $s_t\in\mathcal{S}$. We benchmark dictionary learning architectures on two special cases of this family.

The first setting is a product of $N$ independent two-state chains, with $\mathcal{S} = \{0, 1\}^N$, latent equal to emission ($s_t=\mathbf{a}_t$), and a transition matrix that factorizes per-feature, $T^{(\mathbf{a})}_{s, s'} = \prod_{i=1}^N T^{(a_i)}_{s_i,\,s'_i}$. Each per-feature chain is parameterized by a stationary firing probability $\pi$ and lag-1 autocorrelation $\rho$, with $\Pr(0\to 1)=\pi(1-\rho)$ and $\Pr(1\to 0)=(1-\pi)(1-\rho)$, and is initialized at its stationary distribution $\mathrm{Bernoulli}(\pi)$. We use $N=20$ features at $d=40$, $T=64$, $n_{\mathrm{seqs}}=4096$ sequences resampled with replacement during training, a shared $\pi=0.5$, and $\rho$ assigned from five levels $\{0.0, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 0.9\}$ in four-feature groups, so the dataset spans the full range from i.i.d.\ firing to strongly temporally persistent firing. Because the latent equals the emission in this setting, the global ground truth collapses to the local feature directions, and an architecture is scored by how well its decoder columns recover $\mathcal{F}$.

The second setting separates latent from emission. The latent space is $\mathcal{S}=\{0,1\}^K$ with $K=10$ independent two-state hidden chains $h_{1:K}$ each at $(\pi=0.05, \rho=0.7)$, sparse and temporally persistent. Emissions are a deterministic function of the current latent: a fixed binary coupling matrix $C\in\{0,1\}^{M\times K}$ with $M=20$ rows, each having exactly $n_{\mathrm{parents}}=2$ parents drawn uniformly without replacement, defines the $\mathsf{OR}$ gate
\[
    s_j(t) = \mathbbm{1}\!\left[\,\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^K C_{ji}\,h_i(t)\geq 1\,\right],
\]
so emission $j$ fires whenever any of its parents is on. The firing mask is $\mathbf{a}_t = (s_1(t),\ldots,s_M(t))\in\{0,1\}^M$ and the observation is $\mathbf{x}_t = \sum_{j=1}^M s_j(t)\,m_{j,t}\,\mathbf{f}_j$ with $d=256$, $T=64$, and the same $n_{\mathrm{seqs}}=4096$. This second setting admits two distinct ground truths: the $M=20$ \emph{emission} directions $\{\mathbf{f}_j\}$, recoverable per position, and the $K=10$ \emph{hidden} directions $\tilde{\mathbf{f}}_i = \mathrm{normalize}(\sum_{j:\,C_{ji}=1} \mathbf{f}_j)$ defined as the normalized mean of each chain's children, which can only be expressed by a decoder that pools across positions.

A $T=5$ sparse-autoencoding dictionary can be parameterized with either block-diagonal cross-position weights (each window position gets its own SAE, sharing a dictionary; \textit{StackedSAE}) or with full-rank cross-position weights ($W_{enc}$ and $W_{dec}$ are dense across the temporal window; \textit{TXC}). Both arms -- StackedSAE and TXC -- hook the post-residual stream of $\texttt{gemma-2-2b-it}$ block $13$, same activation cache, $T=5$ windows, $k=100$ sparsity, $d_{sae}=18,432$ dictionary width. What does additional cross-position capacity in TXC surface qualitatively?

We autointerpert [cite here] every active feature in each arm by running the trained model over a $1,500$-chain sample of the cache and keeping every feature with at least three top-window examples. For each feature we elicit a single-sentence concept explanation via $\texttt{Claude Haiku 4.5}$ [cite here] [see Appendix for the prompt]. Explanations are embedded with $\texttt{sentence-transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2}$, projected to 2D with UMAP \cite{UMAP_McInnes2018}, and partitioned with HDBSCAN \cite{hdbscan_McInnes2017}. Clusters are labeled lexically by their distinctive content tokens. For per-sentence visualization we pick $32$-token sequences and select the top-$32$ features per arm via an \textit{exclusive score} (each position claims its most-concentrated feature, greedy assignment) [see Appendix for the pseudocode].

On the per-sentence activation maps (Fig. X), TXC features fire as wide as at least $T$-token diagonal bands following the span of the underlying concept, while StackedSAE features fire as isolated single-position spikes co-located with the concept's most informative token. See Appendix for more.

In the explanation embedding (Fig. \ref{fig:txc_umap}), TXC yields $k=15$ well-separated clusters (silhouette $+0.01$) versus StackedSAE's $k=23$ tighter but heavily overlapping clusters (silhouette $−0.20$) (See Appendix). StackedSAE's groupings are concrete entity types (acronyms, dates, geographic markers, sports headlines), while TXC additionally captures discourse-level abstractions (first-person narrative openings, news article datelines, contrast/transition markers). An LLM-judged temporal-coherence score over the cluster labels favors TXC ($6.80$ vs $4.78$). StackedSAE wins on lexical coherence [define it] ($7.08$ vs $6.31$), which is a by-product of the design to memorize narrow token-level patterns at the cost of discourse-level abstraction.

\subsection{Sparse Probing}
% Outline

% Par 1: Why sparse probing?
% The point here is that its a
Sparse probing is a benchmark of how useful a dictionary's features are for predicting human-interpretable concepts in text. The concepts in standard probing panels span a wide range: some --- part-of-speech, punctuation --- are properties of single tokens, while others --- topics, sentiments, named entities --- are properties of extended passages that vary smoothly across a sequence \citep{bhalla2025tsae}. Different dictionary architectures encode different inductive biases --- per-token, cross-layer, or temporal-window --- so sparse probing is the natural setting in which to measure, concept by concept, which kind of bias proves most useful. Following \citet{kantamneni2025are}, we measure for each concept how accurately a small linear classifier trained on the top-$S$ features (selected by mean activation difference between positive and negative examples) can distinguish examples that exhibit the concept from those that do not. We find in \cref{fig:sparse_probing} that the multi-layer crosscoder narrowly leads, with the temporal crosscoder, the T-SAE, and the per-token TopK SAE clustered closely behind.

% Par 2: Methodology, just re-summarize the josh engels paper
We evaluate on \texttt{google/gemma-2-2b-it} layer~$13$ across the SAEBench multi-task panel of \citet{kantamneni2025are} --- $38$ binary one-vs-rest tasks across $8$ datasets (profession, sentiment, programming language, language, news topic, and two cross-token coreference tasks). Per-token architectures (TopK SAE, TFA) mean-pool latents over the $32$-token tail of each prompt; window architectures (TXC-base, TXC-pro, T-SAE, MLC) slide a width-$T$ window at stride $1$ across the same tail and mean-pool the per-window latents. Every architecture is trained at the same expected per-token $L_0\!=\!20$ --- each arch's internal TopK budget is scaled by its temporal unit --- so cross-architecture differences reflect feature quality rather than admitted bits per token. We additionally train TXC-base at $T \in \{5, 10, 20\}$ to test whether longer temporal context monotonically improves probing. Following \citet{kantamneni2025are}, the top-$S$ features are selected on the train split only by $|\mu_{y=1} - \mu_{y=0}|$ and an $\ell_1$-regularised logistic regression with $C\!=\!1$ is fitted on those features; we sweep $k_{\mathrm{feats}} \in \{5, 10, 20, 40, 80, 160, 320, 640\}$, average per-task ROC-AUC over $3$ training seeds, and report a per-arch summary $\overline{\mathrm{AUC}}$ as the trapezoidal integral of mean SAEBench AUC over $\log_2 k_{\mathrm{feats}}$ divided by the sweep span.

% Par 3: Results
\Cref{fig:sparse_probing_gemma_it} ranks the eight cells. MLC leads at $\overline{\mathrm{AUC}} = 0.907$, followed by a tight TXC-base $T$-sweep cluster at $0.899$--$0.902$, T-SAE and TXC-pro at $0.897$--$0.899$, and TopK SAE behind at $0.886$. The $\sim\!0.014$ summary-AUC gap from the temporal family to the per-token TopK baseline is well outside the per-cell seed-$\sigma$ ($\leq 0.002$ for every cell except TFA). The unaggregated curves in \cref{fig:sparse_probing_curves_gemma_it} show the cluster separating from TopK SAE by $k_{\mathrm{feats}} \geq 20$ and saturating near $k_{\mathrm{feats}} = 160$, with the three TXC-base $T$ variants showing a small monotonic improvement at $k_{\mathrm{feats}} \leq 40$ that flattens at $k_{\mathrm{feats}} \geq 80$ --- evidence that the temporal benefit on this task saturates inside a five-token window. TFA underperforms the locked set at $\overline{\mathrm{AUC}} = 0.764$, attributable to its paper-faithful $B\!=\!32$ batch size; we report the full TFA curve in \cref{app:c3-full-curves} and treat its gap as a batch-size confound rather than a feature-quality finding.

% Par 4: takeaway
The story sparse probing tells lines up with the steering result of \cref{sec:backtracking}: window-aggregated features capture the multi-token concepts that per-token features miss, and the temporal family wins on a static probing task that has no causal structure of its own. The within-family flatness --- MLC, all three TXC-base $T$ variants, T-SAE, and TXC-pro clustered within $0.010$ summary AUC --- suggests that at the locked sparsity the benefit comes from \emph{having a temporal window at all}, not from any specific architectural choice within the family.

Reasoning models exhibit an emergent behavior, sometimes called backtracking, in which the model abandons an in-progress chain of inference and resumes along an alternative path \cite{ward2025reasoning,muennighoff2025s1}. The signature is multi-token: the model commits to a partial argument, registers an inconsistency, and verbalizes a reversal (``wait,'' ``actually''). \citet{ward2025reasoning} show that this behavior can be reliably induced by a difference-of-means steering vector mined from the \emph{base} model and applied to its reasoning-finetuned counterpart at inference. Because the signature spans positions, a per-token sparse dictionary cannot represent it. Backtracking is therefore a natural testbed for the temporal-window inductive bias of \cref{sec:txc}.

We pair the steering-vector approach of \citet{ward2025reasoning} with our own evaluation protocol. Vectors are mined from \texttt{meta-llama/Llama-3.1-8B} and applied at residual-stream layer $10$ on \texttt{deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B}. The cohort is $61$ MATH-500 questions \cite{hendrycks2021math}, $31$ truly-wrong and $30$ originally-correct. For each question we cut the unsteered continuation at $25\%$ of its length and resample under steering at each of $25$ magnitudes (the \textit{cut25} protocol; \cref{app:c7-cut25}). All six architectures train on the same Llama-3.1-8B layer-$10$ activation cache at $d_{\text{SAE}} = 32{,}768$.

We report two metrics. \emph{Inducement} measures whether the architecture's mined feature direction acts as a causal lever for genuine backtracking when used as a steering vector. We track it as the raw count $gc(a, m)$ and as the baseline-corrected lift
\begin{equation}
    \Delta gc(a, m) \,=\, \frac{1}{|\mathcal{Q}|} \sum_{q \in \mathcal{Q}} \bigl[\, gc(a, q, m) - gc(a, q, 0) \bigr],
    \label{eq:delta-gc}
\end{equation}
where $gc(a, q, m)$ is a Sonnet-$4.6$ count of genuine backtracking events (\cref{app:c7-judge}). \emph{Detection} is a sparse-probe PR-AUC at top-$S$ features under $5$-fold GroupKFold by question (\cref{app:c7-pr-auc-full}).

\Cref{fig:c7-headline} summarizes both axes. \cseveenTopGcCell{} leads inducement at $gc \approx \cseveenTopGcPeak{}$ ($m = \cseveenTopGcPeakMag{}$), against the shared unsteered floor of $gc \approx \cseveenTopGcBaseline{}$, for a baseline-corrected lift of $\Delta gc_{\text{peak}} = \cseveenTopDelta{}$. \cseveenTopPrAucCell{} leads detection at PR-AUC$\,=\,\cseveenTopPrAuc{}$, comfortably above the class-prior chance level of $0.12$. The two TXC-base batch-size variants peak at the same magnitude (\cref{fig:c7-inducement-app}), suggesting that the architecture identifies a reproducible feature direction whose causal effect is stable under batch-size scaling. Architecture ordering is preserved between PR-AUC and ROC-AUC and across the full $S$ grid (\cref{tab:c7-pr-auc}). Because detection does not depend on judge subjectivity or magnitude calibration, it serves as an independent corroboration of the inducement story.

Limitations (single-seed headline, judge $\kappa$ deferred, batch-size effects, alternative steering protocols) are discussed in \cref{app:c7-seeds,app:c7-bs-256,app:c7-judge,app:c7-steering-variants}.

\subsection{Decomposing HH-RLHF Preference Data}
\label{sec:rlhf}
% Criticism: justification is somewhat circular: T-SAE argued temporal features matter on this task, we re-run the same protocol.

% Par 1: Motivation, why this is a good temporal task, our key result.
The Anthropic HH-RLHF dataset \cite{bai2022training} pairs each prompt with a preferred (\texttt{chosen}) and dispreferred (\texttt{rejected}) assistant response. \citet{bhalla2025tsae} argue that the response-level signal that distinguishes the two sides is intrinsically multi-token (e.g.\ \emph{the assistant declines}, \emph{the assistant requests clarification}, \emph{the assistant disclaims a capability}), and use HH-RLHF as the dataset-decomposition case study that motivates temporal features in the first place. We therefore include HH-RLHF as a real-world test of feature interpretability and re-run the §4.5 / Appendix B.1 protocol from \cite{bhalla2025tsae} on our locked architectures. We find that the paper-faithful T-SAE configuration ($k\!=\!20$) recovers the cleanest set of semantic top features, with our temporal crosscoder underperforming on this dataset-decomposition metric. This is an honest negative for TXC: window aggregation appears to amplify length-correlated signals that the per-token paper-faithful T-SAE training regime is specifically tuned to suppress.

% Par 2: Method
% Important parts:
% a) we reproduce T-SAE §4.5 / Appendix B.1 verbatim, except for the architecture set being our locked one rather than theirs.
% b) we evaluate at the architecture's native sparsity, not a uniform k.
% c) the length-Pearson-r threshold for semantic vs spurious is from the paper.
% d) we autointerpret with Claude Haiku 4.5, not GPT-4o.
We follow \citet[§4.5, App.\ B.1]{bhalla2025tsae}: we take the first $N\!=\!1000$ \texttt{(chosen, rejected)} pairs from the \texttt{harmless-base} train split of \texttt{Anthropic/hh-rlhf}, tokenise each side separately through Gemma-2-2b base with \texttt{max\_length}$=256$, mask the differing assistant response via character-level longest-common-prefix between the two sides, and capture residual-stream activations at layer $12$. As a sanity check, we reproduce the response-length t-test from the paper's Appendix B.1: rejected responses are longer than chosen ($36.23$ vs $28.57$ tokens, paired $t$-test $p\!=\!9.76\!\times\!10^{-10}$, matching the paper's $p\!\approx\!9\!\times\!10^{-10}$). For each architecture we encode every captured activation, average each feature's activation over response tokens only, and rank features by $\mu_{\text{rejected}} - \mu_{\text{chosen}}$ (the §4.5 metric). Per top-$K$ feature, we compute the Pearson correlation between $(\text{rejected\_act} - \text{chosen\_act})$ and $(\text{rejected\_len} - \text{chosen\_len})$ across the $1000$ pairs, and classify each feature as \emph{semantic} ($|r| < 0.2$), \emph{mixed} ($0.2 \leq |r| < 0.5$), or \emph{length-spurious} ($|r| \geq 0.5$). Each of the top features is autointerpreted via Claude Haiku 4.5 [cite here] on its top-$5$ max-activating contexts (response tokens only); the resulting one-sentence concept labels are used to qualitatively check that the semantic features the analysis flags are in fact alignment-relevant.

% Par 3: Results
\Cref{fig:c1-hh-rlhf-summary} reports the top-$20$ classification per architecture. The paper-faithful T-SAE configuration ($k\!=\!20$) leads with $14/20$ semantic features and zero length-spurious features, accounting for $63\%$ of the total $|\mu_{\text{rejected}} - \mu_{\text{chosen}}|$ mass. Both per-token architectures trained at our larger $k_{\text{win}}\!=\!500$ convention (TopK SAE and T-SAE at matched $k$) collapse onto each other at $\sim\!10$ semantic features and $50\%$ semantic mass: the contrastive-loss benefit visible at $k\!=\!20$ is largely washed out by the denser activation budget. TXC at $T\!=\!5$, $k_{\text{win}}\!=\!500$ underperforms the per-token cells with $7/20$ semantic features and $3$ length-spurious features (vs $0$--$1$ for the per-token cells), while having the largest total $|\mu_{\text{rejected}} - \mu_{\text{chosen}}|$ magnitudes ($\max\!=\!1.52$ vs $1.47$ for T-SAE $k\!=\!500$). A natural reading is that TXC's window aggregation integrates over the equivalent of a small conversational unit, amplifying both semantic content and length-driven artefacts (more chances per window for sentence terminators, conversational fillers, or quoted material), whereas the paper-faithful T-SAE training is specifically tuned to suppress the length-driven channel.

% Par 4: Takeaway
The dataset-decomposition picture here runs in the opposite direction from the steering case study of \cref{sec:backtracking}: where windowed features are a causal lever for interventions that span tokens, they are a liability for ranking-based feature interpretability on a static preference dataset. We treat this as evidence that the same temporal inductive bias has different sign on causal-intervention vs static-decomposition tasks, and read the two case studies together rather than in isolation.
