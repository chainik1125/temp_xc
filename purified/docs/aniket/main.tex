\begin{abstract}
Dictionary learning methods - such as Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs) and crosscoders - decompose model activations into human interpretable building blocks. We introduce \textit{temporal crosscoders}, a simple and flexible framework for feature discovery in Large Language Models (LLMs) at multiple scales. We evaluate temporal crosscoders across a panel of synthetic and real-world tasks and show that they match or exceed existing architectures across most tasks.
% Include two most choice examples
In the synthetic setting, we generalize the existing benchmarking suite to appropriately evaluate ground truth temporal features, and show that temporal crosscoders exceed state-of-the-art architectures in recovering temporal features. In the real world setting, we evaluate temporal crosscoders as a tool for both intervention and detection across a panel of sparse probing, reasoning, deception, and alignment tasks.
% Need some examples.
Our results establish temporal crosscoders as a flexible
% state-of-the-art
framework for feature discovery, both local and temporal.
\end{abstract}

Synthetic datasets make it possible to evaluate feature recovery against known ground-truth features \cite{chanin2025synthetic}. Existing synthetic benchmarks define feature activations independently at each sequence position, so the ground-truth feature structure is local. To benchmark temporal architectures, we generalize this setting by making the feature-firing pattern a stochastic process across sequence positions.

To do so, consider a synthetic activation sequence
\[
    \mathbf{X}=(\mathbf{x}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{x}_T),
    \qquad
    \mathbf{x}_t\in\mathbb{R}^d,
\]
defined over a temporal window of length $T$. A synthetic setting is specified by three pieces of data. First, a set of $N$ ground-truth feature directions
\[
    \mathcal{F}=\{\mathbf{f}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{f}_N\},
    \qquad
    \mathbf{f}_i\in\mathbb{R}^d,
\]
constructed by gradient-based orthogonalisation against a target pairwise cosine similarity. Second, a probability distribution $P_T$ over binary feature-firing masks
\[
    A=(\mathbf{a}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{a}_T)\in(\{0,1\}^N)^T,
    \qquad
    \mathbf{a}_t=(a_{t,1},\ldots,a_{t,N}),
\]
where $a_{t,i}=1$ indicates that feature $i$ fires at position $t$. Third, a distribution $M_T$ over feature magnitudes
\[
    M=(\mathbf{m}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{m}_T),
    \qquad
    \mathbf{m}_t=(m_{t,1},\ldots,m_{t,N}),
\]
which we take to be i.i.d.\ folded-normal across feature index and sequence position, $m_{t,i}=|\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^2)|$ with $\mu=1.0$ and $\sigma=0.15$. The synthetic activation at position $t$ is then
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{x}_t=\sum_{i=1}^N a_{t,i}\,m_{t,i}\,\mathbf{f}_i.
    \label{eq:synthetic-activation}
\end{equation}
The conventional, local synthetic setting is recovered as the special case in which $P_T$ factorises across sequence positions, $P_T(\mathbf{a}_{1:T})=\prod_{t=1}^T p_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbf{a}_t)$. Local feature correlations are encoded by the one-position marginal over $\mathbf{a}_t$; temporal correlations are encoded by the joint distribution over $\mathbf{a}_{1:T}$.

We benchmark dictionary-learning architectures on two synthetic settings.

\paragraph{(C1) Independent two-state Markov support.}
Each of $N=20$ features carries its own independent two-state Markov chain controlling whether the feature fires at each position. Each chain is parameterised by a stationary firing probability $\pi$ and a lag-1 autocorrelation $\rho$, with transition probabilities
\[
    \Pr(0\to 1) = \pi(1-\rho),
    \qquad
    \Pr(1\to 0) = (1-\pi)(1-\rho),
\]
and is initialised from its stationary distribution $\mathrm{Bernoulli}(\pi)$. We use a shared $\pi=0.5$ across all features, and assign $\rho$ from five levels $\rho\in\{0.0,\,0.3,\,0.5,\,0.7,\,0.9\}$ with four features per level, so the dataset spans the full range from i.i.d.\ firing ($\rho=0$) to strongly temporally persistent firing ($\rho=0.9$). We use $d=40$ and $T=64$.

\paragraph{(C2) Coupled features via OR-gated emission.}
We generate $K=10$ independent hidden two-state Markov chains $h_{1:K}$ and $M=20$ emission features $s_{1:M}$ connected by a fixed binary coupling matrix $C\in\{0,1\}^{M\times K}$. The hidden chains are uniform with $\pi=0.05$ and $\rho=0.7$. The coupling matrix is sampled once at dataset construction so each emission $j$ has exactly $n_{\mathrm{parents}}=2$ parents drawn uniformly without replacement, and the emission rule is the OR gate
\[
    s_j(t) = \mathbb{1}\!\left[\,\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^K C_{ji}\,h_i(t)\geq 1\,\right],
\]
so emission $j$ fires whenever any of its parent hidden states is on. The observation is $\mathbf{x}_t = \sum_{j=1}^M s_j(t)\,m_{j,t}\,\mathbf{f}_j$ with $\mathbf{f}_j\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ the orthogonal emission directions and $d=256$. This setting has a genuine separation between local and global ground truth: the $M=20$ emission directions $\{\mathbf{f}_j\}$ are the local ground truth, while the global ground truth is the set of $K=10$ hidden-state directions
\[
    \tilde{\mathbf{f}}_i \;=\; \mathrm{normalize}\!\left(\textstyle\sum_{j:\,C_{ji}=1} \mathbf{f}_j\right),
    \qquad i=1,\ldots,K,
\]
each defined as the normalised mean of its children's emission directions.

\paragraph{Metrics.}
We report reconstruction NMSE
\[
    \mathrm{NMSE} \;=\; \mathbb{E}\!\left[\|\mathbf{x}-\hat{\mathbf{x}}\|^2\right] \,/\, \mathbb{E}\!\left[\|\mathbf{x}\|^2\right]
\]
and a feature-recovery AUC against a ground-truth direction set $\mathcal{G}=\{\mathbf{g}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{g}_K\}$. For each $\mathbf{g}_k\in\mathcal{G}$ we match its best decoder column by absolute cosine similarity, $c_k=\max_j |\langle \mathbf{g}_k,\mathbf{w}_j\rangle|$ (with both vectors normalised), and integrate the fraction-recovered curve over a threshold sweep $\tau\in[0,1]$:
\[
    \mathrm{AUC}(\mathcal{G}) \;=\; \int_0^1 \frac{1}{|\mathcal{G}|}\sum_{k}\mathbb{1}\!\left[c_k\geq\tau\right]\,\mathrm{d}\tau.
\]
For C1, $\mathcal{G}$ is the $N=20$ ground-truth feature set $\mathcal{F}$ (we report this single AUC, plus $\overline{c}=\mathrm{mean}(c_k)$). For C2 we report both an emission AUC (eAUC) against the $M=20$ emission directions $\{\mathbf{f}_j\}$ and a global AUC (gAUC) against the $K=10$ hidden directions $\{\tilde{\mathbf{f}}_i\}$. All metrics are computed on a held-out evaluation batch and reported per training seed $\{1,2,42\}$ with mean and standard deviation across seeds.

\paragraph{Motivation.}
Reasoning models exhibit an emergent behaviour, sometimes called \textit{backtracking}, in which the model abandons an in-progress chain of inference and resumes along an alternative path \cite{ward2025reasoning,muennighoff2025s1}. The behavioural signature is intrinsically multi-token: the model commits to a partial argument, registers an inconsistency, and verbalises a reversal (``wait,'' ``actually''). \citet{ward2025reasoning} show that this behaviour can be reliably induced by a difference-of-means steering vector mined from the \emph{base} model and applied to its reasoning-finetuned counterpart at inference. Because the signature spans token positions, a per-token sparse dictionary, by construction, has access only to a single position; this makes backtracking a natural testbed for the temporal-window inductive bias of \cref{sec:txc}.

\paragraph{Setup.}
We adopt the steering-vector approach of \citet{ward2025reasoning} and pair it with our own evaluation protocol. Vectors are mined from \texttt{meta-llama/Llama-3.1-8B} and applied during continuation on \texttt{deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B} at residual-stream layer $10$ (the optimal hookpoint per \citet[App. B.1]{ward2025reasoning}). The cohort is $61$ MATH-500 \cite{hendrycks2021math} questions ($31$ truly-wrong + $30$ originally-correct). For each question we cut the unsteered baseline continuation at $25\%$ of its length and resample under steering at each of $25$ magnitudes (the \textit{cut25} protocol; \cref{app:c7-cut25}). All six architectures are trained on the same Llama-3.1-8B layer-$10$ activation cache at $d_{\text{SAE}} = 32{,}768$. We report two metrics. The first, \textit{inducement}, measures whether the architecture's mined feature direction acts as a causal lever for genuine backtracking when used as a steering vector. We report it both as a directly-readable raw count $gc(a, m)$ (mean genuine backtracking events per question at magnitude $m$, plotted in \cref{fig:c7-gc-at-peak} for each architecture's optimal magnitude) and as a baseline-corrected lift
\begin{equation}
    \Delta gc(a, m) \,=\, \frac{1}{|\mathcal{Q}|} \sum_{q \in \mathcal{Q}} \bigl[\, gc(a, q, m) - gc(a, q, 0) \bigr],
    \label{eq:delta-gc}
\end{equation}
where $gc(a, q, m)$ is a Sonnet-$4.6$ count of genuine backtracking events (\cref{app:c7-judge}). The second, \textit{detection}, is a sparse-probe PR-AUC at top-$S$ features under $5$-fold GroupKFold by question (\cref{app:c7-pr-auc-full}).

\paragraph{Results.}
\Cref{fig:c7-gc-at-peak} reports the headline inducement number, and \cref{fig:backtracking} summarises both the inducement curve and the detection axis. Per-architecture peak $\Delta gc$, peak magnitude, and the full PR-AUC and ROC-AUC tables at $S \in \{1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32\}$ are tabulated in \cref{tab:c7-pr-auc} (\cref{app:c7-pr-auc-full}).

    \item Further work: long context windows. Tensor Network methods could do this!
